Most pool owners in Cyprus have heard the word "winterise" and assumed it doesn't apply here. After all, the island doesn't freeze. You can still swim in October in Limassol, and plenty of people take a dip well into November. But ignoring your pool between November and March is a mistake, even if the air temperature never drops below ten degrees. Off-season neglect is one of the most common causes of expensive repairs we see every spring. What actually needs doing is different from what pool owners in northern Europe do, but it still needs doing.

## Cyprus Is Not a Cold Climate — But Winter Still Changes Everything

The obvious point first: you will not be draining your pool to prevent pipes bursting. Ground frost is not a realistic concern across most of the island, and the kind of full closure that pool owners in Germany or the UK carry out simply isn't necessary or appropriate here.

What does change in winter is usage. Most residential pools and a large share of holiday rental pools drop to zero swimmers from roughly November through March. That reduced usage creates its own problems. Without bathers disturbing the water, debris sits longer. Without the same evaporation rates as July, chemical dosing habits built around summer conditions can easily overshoot. And without anyone looking at the pool every day, small problems — a slow leak, a cracked grout line, a filter that's starting to struggle — go unnoticed for months.

The other factor is weather. Cypriot winters bring rain, strong winds, and periodic dust events from the south. A single Saharan dust storm can drop enough fine particulate into a pool to overwhelm a filter that was running fine in September. Hard water, which is characteristic of the island due to the limestone geology of the Troodos foothills and coastal aquifers, doesn't stop being hard in January. Scale still builds on heat exchangers, tile lines, and pump fittings all year round.

## What Off-Season Pool Care Actually Involves

### Water chemistry maintenance

This is the part that gets skipped most often. Owners assume that because no one is swimming, the water can just sit there. It cannot. Algae doesn't hibernate. Calcium carbonate scaling continues whenever the saturation index tips out of balance. And if you're heating the pool for occasional winter use — which some villa owners and Airbnb hosts do — you're running a heat exchanger through water that may not have been properly treated since September.

During winter months, test your water at least once every two weeks. Adjust pH, total alkalinity, and calcium hardness as needed. The target ranges don't change: pH between 7.2 and 7.6, total alkalinity 80 to 120 ppm, calcium hardness 200 to 400 ppm. Chlorine demand drops when UV intensity drops, so your dosing will likely come down, but it won't go to zero.

### Filtration and circulation

Run your pump. Shorter cycles are fine — four to six hours per day is usually adequate for an unheated, unused residential pool in winter. But stopping circulation entirely is a common cause of stagnant water, algae blooms, and debris settling on the pool floor in ways that stain.

Check your filter pressure every few weeks and backwash when needed. If you have a sand filter, winter is actually a good time to consider whether the sand needs replacing. Sand that has been in place for more than five years tends to channelling, which means water passes through gaps rather than being filtered. You won't necessarily see this as a problem in summer when you're dosing heavily, but in winter with lighter chemistry it becomes apparent quickly.

### Physical inspection

With the pool quieter and calmer, winter is the right time to look carefully at things you might miss in summer. Check grout lines around tiles, particularly at and just below the waterline — this area takes the most chemical and UV stress over the summer. Look at the skimmer baskets, the pump lid o-ring, the backwash valve, and any visible pipe fittings for cracks or deterioration. In Cyprus, the combination of high summer UV and calcium-rich water accelerates wear on seals and flexible joints. Catching a minor seal failure in January is considerably cheaper than finding a slow leak has been losing water for two months.

### Pool covers

A quality cover significantly reduces debris load, slows evaporation, and limits algae growth by blocking light. In Cyprus, a solid or mesh safety cover is worth the investment if the pool will genuinely sit unused for several months. Leaf covers — the lightweight type secured with water bags — are a lower-cost option that handles the main debris problem without addressing algae or evaporation. Solar covers are less practical in winter because you're not trying to gain heat during cold months, but they do work if the pool is still being used occasionally and you want to retain overnight warmth.

## What You Can Safely Leave Alone

You do not need to lower the water level below skimmers unless the pool is truly out of use and exposed to any risk of overflow from winter rain — and even then, dropping the level by thirty to forty centimetres is usually enough. You do not need to blow out the pipes or remove fittings. You do not need to add any special "winterising" chemicals sold in northern European markets. Most of those products are formulated around the assumption that the pool will sit completely static and cold for months. In Cyprus, your water temperature rarely drops below 12 to 14 degrees Celsius even in January, and some light circulation will continue.

## Off-Season Care Pays Off in Spring

The pools that come to us in the worst condition each April are almost always the ones where the owner has treated winter as a closed season. Green water, heavy scale deposits, stained plaster, and struggling equipment are all avoidable. A consistent but lighter maintenance routine through winter keeps your pool ready to use when the weather shifts and means spring commissioning is a matter of adjusting chemistry rather than an expensive recovery project.
