Cyprus summers are brutal on pool water. Between June and September, outdoor temperatures regularly exceed 38°C, UV radiation is intense, and evaporation rates climb fast. If you're managing a pool on the island — whether it's your own home, a villa, or a holiday rental — this combination creates real chemical challenges that catch a lot of owners off guard. Here's what's actually happening to your water and what you can do about it.

## Why Heat Destabilises Pool Chemistry So Quickly

Water temperature is one of the most underestimated factors in pool maintenance. When your pool sits at 30–32°C for weeks on end, several things happen simultaneously.

Chlorine burns off significantly faster. Free chlorine is photolabile, meaning UV light breaks it down. In July and August in Cyprus, you can lose a substantial portion of your free chlorine within hours of dosing if you're using unstabilised chlorine without adequate cyanuric acid levels. This isn't a minor inefficiency — it means your pool can go from adequately sanitised to vulnerable in a single day.

At the same time, warmer water accelerates bacterial and algal growth. The organisms that cause green, cloudy, or slimy water reproduce far more rapidly at 30°C than at 20°C. So the window between a balanced pool and a problem pool is much narrower in summer than it is in spring or autumn.

Heat also increases evaporation. A Cyprus pool in peak summer can lose 5–8mm of water per day, and as water evaporates, dissolved minerals and chemicals become more concentrated. This raises your total dissolved solids (TDS) and contributes to the imbalances discussed below.

## The Hard Water Problem Is Worse Than Most People Realise

Cyprus has some of the hardest mains water in Europe. The geology of the island, particularly the limestone-dominant formations across much of Limassol, Paphos, and Nicosia, means the water supply is naturally high in calcium. Calcium hardness in many areas runs well above 400 ppm, and in some rural districts, significantly higher.

This matters in summer for a specific reason: as water temperature rises, calcium carbonate becomes less soluble. It wants to come out of solution and deposit itself on your pool surfaces, your pump, your heat exchanger if you have one, and your filter media. The Langelier Saturation Index, which measures the scaling or corrosive tendency of water, shifts unfavourably as temperature climbs. A pool that was chemically balanced in April can be actively scaling by July without any change in your dosing routine.

### What to Watch For

Scale appears first on tile grout lines and around waterline tiles. If you're seeing a white or grey crust building up, that's calcium carbonate deposition. It's not cosmetic — left long enough, it becomes very difficult to remove without acid washing and can damage tile adhesion.

Top up your pool with fresh water regularly to dilute mineral concentration, keep your pH between 7.2 and 7.4, and consider having your Langelier Index calculated as part of a professional water test if you're dealing with persistent scaling.

## Algae in Cyprus: Conditions Are Almost Ideal for It

Green algae is the most common type seen in Cyprus pools. It colonises quickly in warm, poorly sanitised water and can turn a clear pool visibly green within 48 hours under the right conditions. Mustard algae (a yellowish-green strain) is also present on the island and is more resistant to standard chlorine levels.

The factors that combine to create algae problems in a Cyprus summer are worth listing plainly:

- High water temperature promoting rapid growth
- Intense UV degrading your chlorine before it can work
- Dust storms from North Africa depositing organic material and phosphates into the water
- High bather loads in holiday rentals reducing chlorine faster
- Irregular maintenance schedules, especially during August when many owners are away

### Staying Ahead of Algae

Prevention is considerably cheaper and easier than treatment. A green pool can require repeated shocking, brushing, filter backwashing, and days of recovery time. Prevention costs a fraction of that.

Stabiliser (cyanuric acid) levels should be maintained between 30 and 50 ppm. Below this, your chlorine has no UV protection. Above 80 ppm, the stabiliser itself begins to inhibit chlorine's effectiveness, a condition sometimes called chlorine lock.

Shock your pool weekly during June, July, and August rather than fortnightly. Use a good-quality calcium hypochlorite or stabilised chlorine shock, and dose in the evening so the product isn't immediately degraded by midday sun.

Run your filtration system for at least 10–12 hours per day in summer. Filtration alone won't kill algae, but it keeps water moving, prevents stagnation, and removes the fine particles that algae feed on.

Brush pool walls and floor weekly, even if the water looks clear. Algae begins as an invisible biofilm on surfaces before it becomes visible in the water column. Brushing disrupts early-stage colonisation before it takes hold.

After any significant dust event, check your phosphate levels. North African dust storms are a regular occurrence in Cyprus and introduce phosphates, which are a primary nutrient source for algae. Phosphate removers are available and worth using after heavy dust deposits.
