If you've noticed a white, chalky crust forming around your waterline, on your tiles, or around your jets and fittings, you're dealing with calcium scale. It's one of the most common problems in Cyprus pools, and it's almost entirely down to the water itself. This post explains what causes it, how to treat it, and what you can do to slow it down.

## Why Cyprus Water Causes Scale Problems

Cyprus has some of the hardest tap water in Europe. The island's geology is dominated by limestone and chalk formations, and groundwater picks up calcium carbonate as it moves through the rock. By the time water reaches your pool, it often arrives with a calcium hardness level well above 400 parts per million. Recommended levels for pool water sit between 200 and 400 ppm, so many pools here start off out of range before you've even added a single chemical.

The problem doesn't stop at source water. Cyprus pools are exposed to intense UV radiation and summer air temperatures that regularly exceed 35°C. This causes rapid evaporation, which concentrates the minerals already in the water. As water evaporates, calcium stays behind. Refill with more hard water, and the concentration climbs again. Repeat this cycle over a summer, and you can end up with calcium hardness levels above 600 or even 700 ppm, at which point scale deposits become aggressive and difficult to manage.

High pH compounds the issue significantly. When pH drifts above 7.8, calcium carbonate becomes less soluble and drops out of solution onto whatever surface it can find: tiles, plaster, heat exchanger surfaces, pipework. This is why pH control isn't just about swimmer comfort or chlorine efficiency. In Cyprus, it's a front-line defence against scale.

## What Scale Actually Does to Your Pool

Scale is more than a cosmetic annoyance. Left untreated, it creates real operational problems.

### Surface Damage

On plaster or pebble finish pools, calcium deposits roughen the surface over time. A rough surface is harder to clean and gives algae more places to take hold. On tiled pools, scale builds up in grout lines and behind loose tiles, accelerating grout deterioration.

### Equipment Damage

Scale inside a heat pump or gas heater significantly reduces efficiency, because calcium is an effective insulator. A scaled heat exchanger has to work harder to achieve the same water temperature, which increases running costs and wear. Scale buildup inside pipework and around fittings can restrict flow enough to put strain on the pump.

### Filtration Issues

Calcium deposits can also coat the laterals inside a sand filter or clog a cartridge filter element, reducing filtration performance over time.

## How to Treat Existing Scale

### Waterline Tile Deposits

For light to moderate scale on tiles, a diluted acid-based tile cleaner applied with a stiff brush is the standard approach. Many pool supply shops in Cyprus stock dedicated scale removers. Always lower the water level slightly below the tile line before treating, and rinse thoroughly before refilling. Wear gloves and eye protection. Muriatic acid is effective for heavier deposits but needs to be handled carefully and used in well-ventilated conditions.

For scale that has been building up for years, mechanical removal may be necessary — glass bead blasting or similar methods — which is work best left to professionals.

### In-Water Treatment

If your calcium hardness is very high and scale is forming across the pool surface rather than just at the waterline, you have two main options. The first is partial or full dilution: draining a portion of the pool and refilling with fresher water to bring hardness down to a workable level. The second is using a sequestering agent, sometimes called a chelating agent or scale inhibitor. These chemicals bind to calcium ions and keep them in suspension rather than letting them precipitate onto surfaces. They don't reduce calcium hardness readings, but they do reduce scaling behaviour. In Cyprus conditions, a sequestering agent used regularly throughout summer can make a meaningful difference.

### Lowering pH

If pH is regularly sitting above 7.8, address this first. Bring it down to 7.4 to 7.6 and keep it there. Consistent pH management will slow scale formation more reliably than any single treatment.

## Ongoing Prevention in the Cyprus Climate

The most effective prevention is consistent water testing. In summer, when evaporation is high, test calcium hardness every two to three weeks rather than monthly. If levels are climbing past 500 ppm, partial dilution is often the most practical response, particularly before the peak summer heat.

During the Saharan dust events that affect Cyprus periodically, pools accumulate fine particles that can affect water chemistry. Test after any heavy dust deposit and adjust accordingly.

A few practical habits make a real difference over a season: keep pH stable and on the lower end of the acceptable range, use a sequestering agent on a maintenance dose basis, and don't let the pool go untested for long stretches. Scale that's caught early is easy to manage. Scale that's been building for a season or two becomes a significantly bigger job.
